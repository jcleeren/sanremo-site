Who Pays When the AI Saving Does Not Arrive?

IT services providers are signing for AI savings they do not control.

Jan Cleeren · November 2026 · 13 pages

Get the full paper

Executive summary

AI works. Nearly nine in ten organisations use it, yet only 37% report any effect on profit. Much of what is written about AI tries to explain that gap. This paper asks a different question, the one that matters most to companies that sell IT services and software: when AI makes the work cheaper, who keeps the gain, and who carries the risk if the gain does not materialise?

The client takes the gain, and takes it early. In the contracts being signed today, providers commit to AI savings before they have achieved them. In a survey of large companies, buyers expected services delivered with AI to cost 19% less on average, while senior technology leaders put the cost reduction achieved in software development so far at 8%. ISG, which tracks these contracts, wrote in July 2026 that “neither providers nor enterprises have full visibility into how the savings will be achieved”. The difference is a risk, and it sits with the provider.

The practice itself is not new. In the large outsourcing contracts I worked on, a price that fell every year was standard commercial practice. The risk was manageable as long as providers knew the baseline and controlled the source of the saving.

What is new is that the provider no longer controls that source. In application services the saving depends on an AI tool whose cost rises with use, bought from suppliers who guarantee no result and can change their prices. Part of the saving also depends on changes that only the client can make in its own organisation. In infrastructure services, hardware, which became cheaper every year, has become more expensive and can no longer be priced ahead.

None of this is a reason to refuse such contracts. In my view the providers that learn to price this risk, and to state in the contract who carries it, will win work from those that do not.

Even when the plan works, the provider earns less

The paper works through one contract: a managed service for three years, a team of twelve, a margin of 35%, and a provider that uses AI and passes the saving on in a price 5% lower. If everything goes exactly to plan:

Cost to deliver€142,000 lower
Price to the client€219,000 lower
What the provider earns€77,000 lower

The client receives the whole saving and more, and the provider carries the risk. If the price of AI rises, or the productivity gain stays where it is today, the margin falls from 35% to as low as 25%.

The example makes one point only: a provider needs an agreed way to share the gain with its client before it gives the price reduction. The paper names three ways to do that, and shows the full calculation with four outcomes.

Five questions before an AI saving goes into a price

Answer them for your largest contracts and for the bids you have open today.

QuestionA good answerWarning sign
Which of our contracts already contain an AI saving that we have not yet achieved?A list, with the amount per contract and the date by which it must be achievedNobody has such a list
Who approved that figure before the client saw it?Finance and delivery, in a documented reviewThe saving was added to reach the winning price, and nobody in delivery has signed for it
What is the baseline, and how will the result be tested?A measurement of today’s cost and quality, and written acceptance criteria, both agreed with the clientThe gain is measured by asking users for their impression
What does the AI cost us on this contract, and who is responsible for that cost?The cost is tracked every month against a spending limit, and one person is responsible for itNobody can say what the AI cost on this contract last month
What happens when the price of AI, the price of hardware or the rules of a software vendor change?The contract states the assumptions behind the price, and how and when the price is reviewedThe price was built on today’s costs, and the contract is silent on what happens if they change

Each warning sign marks a place where a firm carries AI risk without being paid for it.
